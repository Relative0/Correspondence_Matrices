What the labels mean. Every chart times the same expressions two ways: CM = the expression compiled to a correspondence-matrix DAG, then evaluated; Bitset = the plain expression tree evaluated directly with packed-bitset arithmetic — no CM machinery (previously labeled "raw"). Both sides then have a choice of arithmetic engine: bigint (Python big integers) or words (numpy uint64 vectors). Engines are always matched CM-vs-Bitset, so each pair is an apples-to-apples comparison of the best available implementation on each side.

The one-time setup cost that used to block big formulas

Before any computation runs, a lookup table must be built once per formula size. The old way of building it got catastrophically slow as n grew; the rewritten version produces the exact same table, fast. Log time scale.

⬇ Lower line = faster

Takeaway: at n=20 the old builder took 16 seconds; the rewrite takes 0.12 seconds — about 130× faster, with byte-for-byte identical output. This one fix, not CM itself, is what made formulas beyond 16 variables practical at all. It benefits CM and Bitset equally, since both need the same table.

The head-to-head verdict: CM time ÷ Bitset time

The most direct comparison on this page. For each scenario we divide CM's time by the best Bitset's time on identical formulas. A value of 1.00 means a dead heat; 0.90 means CM took only 90% as long (CM wins); 1.25 means CM took 25% longer (Bitset wins).

Below the dashed 1× line = CM faster · above it = Bitset faster

Takeaway: in the core number-crunching ("kernel" bars, blue and teal) CM is usually at parity to faster on this family, thanks to CM re-using repeated sub-formulas; one words cell is a slight CM loss. In the full end-to-end path a user actually calls ("wrapper", yellow), CM's extra bookkeeping makes it modestly slower through n=24 — ratios of 1.05–1.27 in Audit V3. Why the wrapper slows CM down — and what a 300-formula campaign taught us

The story behind the yellow bars in the head-to-head chart, in plain terms.

What the "wrapper" is. The kernel is the bare number-crunching loop. The wrapper is the complete function a user actually calls: it checks the safety guard, works out which variables still matter, runs the kernel, and packages the answer into a result object. That housekeeping costs a roughly fixed 3–5 microseconds per call, regardless of how big the computation is.

When it matters. For the tiny reduced computations in the head-to-head chart, the kernel itself only takes 2–6 µs — so a 3–5 µs surcharge nearly doubles the bill, and CM ends up 14–26% behind the plain Bitset (which has almost no packaging to do). For big computations the same surcharge vanishes into the noise: at n=22 full output the wrapper costs about 0.5% (84.1 ms with vs 84.6 ms without in this session's run). So: kernel-vs-kernel, CM is the faster method (ratios ~0.6–1.0); it is only the fixed per-call packaging cost that puts the end-to-end CM path behind on microsecond-scale outputs.

What the 300-formula campaign found. An early 8-formula pass had shown CM winning at n=24 (0.84); rerunning with 300 formulas per size (1,500 total, all bit-verified) showed that was sampling luck. The archived robust median was 1.02; Audit V3 found 1.05 on the same population and 1.08–1.09 on fresh seeds. The campaign confirmed the structural effect behind the original hunch, though: formulas that simplify to ≤4 relevant variables put end-to-end CM ahead of the Bitset (0.87–1.0 at larger sizes), because CM's compiled formula shrinks while the Bitset still executes every operation of the original. It also exposed a mis-tuned routing threshold: formulas keeping 8+ relevant variables (~20% of draws) were being sent to a slow fallback path, ~40× behind; raising the threshold from 7 to 16 eliminated that cliff entirely (worst stratum now ~1.2–1.4×), with every output still bit-identical.

How this compares to the older published numbers. Before this work, CM's kernel was genuinely slower than Bitset: the original recursive CM evaluator ran about 1.5–2.5× behind the Bitset on cached small formulas (2.47× at n=4 down to 1.53× at n=16, from the earlier session's tables). Two changes reversed that: compiling CM's formula once into a flat program (the "C1a" evaluator), and this audit's corrected comparison. Note the correction cut both ways: the old benchmark also compared CM against a flawed, too-slow "Bitset", so some older ratios flattered CM. With the flawed baseline replaced and both sides given the same engine upgrades, today's honest picture is the one above: CM's kernel modestly ahead, its wrapper modestly behind on tiny outputs.

Reading the results together

The honest headline, in one place.

  • Kernel: CM runs at parity to workload-dependent faster than the best-practice flat Bitset on sharing-bearing formulas; the residual edge tracks DAG compression.
  • Wrapper: for microsecond-scale reduced outputs the public CM wrapper is parity to ~1.4× slower than the fair Bitset baseline. CM's large-n value is the exact reduced representation and the explicit guard, not raw speed.
  • Both sides speed up together: liveness freeing and numpy-words were applied to CM and Bitset alike, so every comparison stays fair.
  • Variance: large-n session CVs ran 7–16%; treat single ratios as ranges, and disclose the repr-3/4 mix behind any median.
